Executive summary

When my lightsaber’s LEDs acted up, I checked the wiring in the handle and changed the code. I liked figuring out why a device I built wasn’t working.

I’m studying electrical and computer engineering at De Anza and Ohlone Colleges. In physics, I trace how current moves through circuits. For my linear algebra honors project, I calculated three voltages two ways and checked the current at each junction. [1]

I want to be an electronics engineer who tests circuit boards for spacecraft instruments. Through NCAS, I learned how engineers at NASA Armstrong test flight hardware and software together. [2] [3] [4] I plan to transfer in fall 2027 and expect to finish my bachelor’s degree in May 2029. [5]

Projects and experiences

A lightsaber with a 3D-printed hilt

Personal build · June 2026 · Arduino Nano and FreeCAD

What I did. I designed a modular hilt in FreeCAD and 3D-printed it. I fit an Arduino Nano and a kill switch into the handle, then checked the wiring and code until the NeoPixel blade lit up. I plan to add motion-triggered effects in the next revision. [5]

NASA connection. At NASA Armstrong, engineers test a flight computer with the hardware it controls. With my lightsaber, a bad command and a power problem could look the same from outside the hilt. Checking the code alone wouldn’t tell me which one to fix. [3]

Getting readings from a device into an app

One Drug · Biomedical Engineering Research Intern · August to September 2026

What I did. At One Drug, I built the second version of the web app for a prototype diagnostic device and a new mobile app. I connected both apps to a Raspberry Pi camera and a temperature sensor. I added checks for dropped connections and invalid readings, then tested the apps before deployment. [5]

NASA connection. Perseverance’s PIXL instrument photographs the patch of rock it tests with X-rays. The picture tells scientists where the reading came from. In our apps, I wanted to be just as clear about which device was sending the data on my screen. [6]

Odontiv, my dental inventory app

Published April 2026 · iOS and iPadOS

What I did. I built Odontiv in Swift and SwiftUI so a dental office could track supplies and capture receipts. The app syncs inventory with CloudKit and exports reports with PDFKit. I released it on the App Store in April 2026. [7]

NASA connection. NASA Armstrong engineers turn a user’s need into a requirement they can test. For Odontiv, I could start with ten syringes, record using two, and check that eight were left. I’d use the same habit of writing down the expected result before testing an instrument. [3]

Technical skills

  • Circuit analysis. In PHYS 141, I use junction and loop rules to follow current and voltage through a circuit. These are the checks I’d start with on a board that isn’t getting power.
  • Linear algebra. In my honors circuit model, substitution and row reduction both gave 3 V, 2 V, and 1 V. I checked those results against the currents at all three nodes. [1]
  • CAD and soldering. I modeled my lightsaber hilt in FreeCAD, printed it, and soldered the wiring. The switch and wires had to fit alongside the controller.
  • Microcontroller programming. I programmed the Arduino Nano to read button presses and change the LED sequence. Following a press all the way to the lights helped me debug the controls.
  • Device testing. At One Drug, I checked device connections and validated incoming readings. That helped me tell whether each app was receiving usable sensor data.
  • Writing testable requirements. At Beyond the Game Health, I turn event flows into requirements the team can test. A registration only counts when the attendee sees a confirmation and the database saves the signup.

NASA career pathway

The job I looked up on O*NET was electronics engineer. It includes designing circuits, testing them, and explaining the results to other people. A bachelor’s degree is a common route into the field. I’m especially interested in the boards that power sensors and carry their readings back to a computer. [2]

Before I transfer

On my next build, I want to keep a short test log. I’ll write down what I expect the circuit to do, measure the supply voltage, and record what actually happens. That would give someone else a way to repeat my test.

Fall 2027 and beyond

I plan to transfer into electrical or computer engineering, study circuits and embedded programming, and design a board I can test myself. For hands-on experience, I’ll look for an instrument or avionics project through NASA’s OSTEM internships. I’ll also check Pathways openings as I work toward an engineering role. [3] [8]
